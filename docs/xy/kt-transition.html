Vortices on a machine with no operating system

We measured a Nobel-Prize-winning phase transition inside a unikernel with no kernel, no libc and no math library — and checked it against a number that theory fixes exactly.

Real output from three runs, not an illustration. Each plate is a 48×48 lattice of spin angles after 15,000 equilibration sweeps. Colour is the angle itself, so a smooth colour field is smooth alignment and a point where every hue meets is a vortex.

The transition that shouldn't exist

Take the Ising model — spins that point up or down — and let each spin point in any direction instead. Something breaks. The Mermin–Wagner theorem says a two-dimensional system like this cannot order at all: the magnetisation is zero at every temperature above absolute zero. There is nothing to break, so by the textbook account there should be no phase transition.

There is one anyway. Kosterlitz and Thouless worked out why in the 1970s and shared the 2016 Nobel Prize for it. The transition isn't about alignment — it's about topology.

Look at the middle plate above. Those marked points are vortices: places where, if you walk in a small circle, the spin angle turns through a full 360°. You cannot smooth one away by nudging spins, only by bringing an opposite one to meet it. At low temperature they exist only in tightly bound pairs. Heat the system and the pairs come apart — and that unbinding is the transition.

The plates show it directly. At T = 0.50 there are no free vortices at all. At T = 0.90 there are twenty. By T = 1.30 there are 246, wandering freely.

What makes this a sharp test

Kosterlitz and Thouless predicted something unusually strict. The stiffness of the spin field — how hard it resists being twisted — does not fall gradually to zero. It drops discontinuously, and the size of that drop is universal:

The jump is exactly 2/π.no coupling constant · no lattice detail · no fitted parameter

The same pure number for every system in this class, whatever it is made of. So the question isn't "did we land near a published value?" It's "does our measured curve cross a line that theory fixes exactly?" — and wherever it crosses is the transition temperature.

The falling curve is the measured stiffness. The straight line is 2T/π, fixed by theory with nothing fitted. Where they cross is the transition.

What we got

Three lattice sizes, against the accepted infinite-lattice value of 0.8929:

LatticeCrossingReading
16 × 160.9395above, as a small lattice must be
32 × 320.9169drifting down, as predicted
64 × 640.9239not resolved — see below

All three land above 0.8929, which is correct: finite lattices always cross high, and they converge only logarithmically in size. That slowness is a known feature of this transition, and it's why pinning it down numerically is genuinely hard rather than merely tedious.

The 64×64 result does not fit the trend, and we're leaving it that way. It should have fallen below the 32×32 value and it came in above. The reason is mundane: we gave it 2.5× fewer sweeps, and near the crossing the curve is steep enough that a little statistical noise moves the intersection a long way. It's an honest limit of how long we ran, not a finding — and quietly dropping the point would have made a cleaner chart and a worse result.

A detail we nearly got wrong

A freestanding unikernel has no math library. No sin, no cos, no exp — and this model is built from cosines, so they had to be written from scratch.

The first version approximated sine with a series truncated at x¹¹, and the comment above it claimed the error was under 1e-11. Checked against a real math library, the actual error was 5.7e-08 — precisely the size of the term that had been left off.

Nothing in the physics would have looked wrong. The curves would have had the same shape, the crossing would have landed in about the same place, and the result would simply have been quietly less accurate than advertised. Two more terms cost two multiplications and bought four orders of magnitude.

FunctionRangeMax error
sin, cos[−20, 20]6.0e-12
exp(−x)[0, 40]6.2e-15

The lesson isn't about trigonometry. It's that a number you didn't measure is a number you don't have — including one in your own comment.

Why run it on a unikernel at all

The whole simulation is one C file on a machine with no operating system, no scheduler and no runtime beneath it. The lattice is 8 bytes per site, so even 128×128 fits in 128 KB — this runs inside the platform's 4 MiB floor, let alone the 16 MiB we gave it.

But the reason that matters isn't size. It's that the run is seeded and deterministic, and we've already shown that the same seed produces bit-identical results on this unikernel and on Linux, from the same source. Monte Carlo results in physics are famously hard to reproduce exactly — seeds go unpublished, generators differ, parallel reductions reorder.

Publish the seed here and the numbers above aren't merely repeatable. Someone can reproduce them exactly — on a different operating system, or on no operating system at all.

2D XY model, periodic boundaries, Metropolis single-spin updates, seeded xoshiro256++. Plates are 48×48 after 15,000 equilibration sweeps. Curve is 64×64. Crossings by linear interpolation between bracketing temperatures. Accepted value T_KT = 0.8929 from high-precision Monte Carlo in the literature.

Vortex and antivortex counts are exactly balanced at every temperature measured — 10 and 10, then 123 and 123 — which is forced by topology on a periodic lattice, and is the check that the counter is right.
